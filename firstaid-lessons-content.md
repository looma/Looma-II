## 1. Snakebite   (`id: snakebite`)

*Illustration idea:* coiled snake beside a swelling ankle

*Source:* WHO 'Snakebite envenoming' health topic and WHO/SEARO Guidelines for the Management of Snakebites (2nd ed.); IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Snakebites'; Tun-Pe et al. field trials on local compression pads for Russell's viper. DECISIONS: limb kept at ~heart level; taught local pressure-PAD method rather than full-limb pressure-immobilisation bandaging; unconscious casualties placed on the left side.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Move away from the snake. Get the person a safe distance from where the bite happened so it can't strike again.  
   *(picture: person stepping back from grass)*
2. Keep the person calm and completely still. Fear and movement speed venom through the body, and most bites are survivable with prompt care.  
   *(picture: person sitting still, calm)*
3. Keep the bitten limb still, immobilised in a comfortable position at roughly heart level. Muscle movement pumps venom into the bloodstream faster.  
   *(picture: limb resting still and supported at heart level)*
4. Gently remove rings, bangles, watches and tight clothing near the bite. Swelling can come fast and trap them.  
   *(picture: removing a bangle from a swelling wrist)*
5. Immobilise the limb with a splint or sling, like a broken bone. A still limb slows venom spread and is the most important thing after staying calm.  
   *(picture: splinted arm in a sling)*
6. If you have a clean cloth and a bandage, place a folded pad (about the size of your palm) over the bite and bandage it firmly like a sprained ankle — snug, not cutting off blood flow. Then still get to hospital fast.  
   *(picture: folded pad bandaged over a bite)*
7. Transport to a hospital with antivenom immediately. Carry the person if you can — do not let them walk.  
   *(picture: person carried toward a health post)*
8. If they vomit or lose consciousness, lay them on their left side to keep the airway clear. Watch their breathing the whole way.  
   *(picture: person on their left side, recovery position)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT apply a tourniquet or tie anything tightly around the limb — it can destroy the limb and doesn't stop venom.
- Do NOT cut, slash, or open the bite wound.
- Do NOT try to suck out or squeeze out the venom.
- Do NOT apply ice, herbs, chemicals, or electric shock to the bite.
- Do NOT give alcohol, coffee, or any stimulant.
- Do NOT give aspirin or ibuprofen for pain — they can worsen bleeding. Paracetamol is safer if pain relief is needed.
- Do NOT chase or try to catch the snake, and do NOT wait to see what happens — go to hospital straight away.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Recognise the two kinds of dangerous venom in Nepal.**
   - *Why:* Cobras and kraits (elapids) carry neurotoxic venom that can cause drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble swallowing and, later, difficulty breathing. Vipers (including Russell's viper and pit vipers, common in Nepal) carry venom that affects blood clotting, causing swelling, bruising and bleeding. Many pit viper bites cause local swelling only. Either way, the first aid is the same — get to hospital.
   - *Tip:* Watch especially for drooping eyelids or trouble breathing — these signal a neurotoxic bite that can turn critical quickly.
2. **Keep the person calm and still.**
   - *Why:* A racing heartbeat and movement pump venom through the body faster. Many bites inject little or no venom, so staying calm genuinely buys time.
   - *Tip:* Reassure them out loud: most snakebites are survivable with prompt hospital care.
3. **Immobilise the bitten limb and keep it still.**
   - *Why:* A still limb slows how fast venom travels — the same logic as splinting a fracture. Keep it in a comfortable position at about heart level, not deliberately raised or lowered.
   - *Tip:* Splint it like a broken bone, but never tight enough to cut off blood flow.
4. **Use a simple pressure pad, not a full-limb pressure bandage.**
   - *Why:* A folded pad over the bite, bandaged firmly like a sprained ankle, is simple to do and helps slow venom for the snakes common in Nepal — including Russell's viper, where local compression pads have been shown to help. A full-limb pressure bandage needs exact, even pressure most people can't judge; applied wrongly it does nothing or causes harm, and it's dangerous over a viper bite.
   - *Tip:* The pad should be snug but never so tight you can't slip a finger under it, and never so tight the fingers or toes go cold or blue.
5. **Get to antivenom fast — it is the only real cure.**
   - *Why:* Antivenom neutralises the toxins and works best early. No cutting, sucking, herb or charm can replace it, and delays cost lives.
   - *Tip:* Note the snake's colour and shape if you safely can — it helps doctors — but never risk a second bite.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** Someone is bitten on the ankle by a snake in a field. What should you do first?
  - [✓] Keep them calm and still, immobilise the limb, and get them to hospital
  - [ ] Tie a tight band above the bite to stop the venom
  - [ ] Cut the wound and suck out the venom
  - *Feedback:* Staying calm and still slows venom spread. Tourniquets, cutting and sucking all cause harm and are never recommended.
- **Q:** Which sign suggests a dangerous neurotoxic bite (cobra or krait)?
  - [✓] Drooping eyelids and trouble swallowing or breathing
  - [ ] A small itch that quickly disappears
  - *Feedback:* Neurotoxic venom attacks the nerves — drooping eyelids, slurred speech and breathing trouble are red flags needing urgent hospital care.

## 2. Severe bleeding   (`id: severe-bleeding`)

*Illustration idea:* hands pressing a folded cloth onto a bleeding forearm

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Severe bleeding'; American Red Cross and British Red Cross bleeding guidance; ILCOR 2020/2025 CoSTR and 'Stop the Bleed' (ABC: Alert–Bleeding–Compress).


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Check the scene is safe and protect yourself. Put on gloves or use a plastic bag between your hand and the blood if you can.  
   *(picture: rescuer putting on gloves)*
2. Call for help or send someone to call. Uncontrolled bleeding is the top preventable cause of death from injury.  
   *(picture: person calling for help)*
3. Find the source of the bleeding. Move clothing aside so you can see the actual wound.  
   *(picture: clothing moved to expose a wound)*
4. Press hard and directly on the wound with a clean cloth or your gloved hand. Firm, direct pressure is the fastest way to stop bleeding.  
   *(picture: hands pressing cloth on wound)*
5. Keep pressing without lifting off. If blood soaks through, add another cloth on top and keep pressing — don't remove the first.  
   *(picture: second cloth added over first)*
6. Help the person lie down and keep them warm and still. This helps prevent shock.  
   *(picture: person lying down, covered with a blanket)*
7. For a limb bleed that won't stop, apply a tourniquet above the wound if you have one and are trained. Note the time it went on.  
   *(picture: tourniquet high on a limb with time noted)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT keep lifting the cloth to peek — it disturbs clotting and restarts the bleeding.
- Do NOT remove an object stuck in the wound — press around it, not on it.
- Do NOT remove blood-soaked cloths — add fresh ones on top.
- Do NOT loosen or remove a tourniquet once applied — leave it for medical staff.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Know how fast bleeding becomes life-threatening.**
   - *Why:* Blood carries oxygen to every organ. An adult has about 5 litres; losing more than about 2 can be fatal, and heavy bleeding can reach that in only a few minutes. That is why 'stop the bleed' comes before almost everything else — faster than waiting for an ambulance.
   - *Tip:* Spurting or pulsing blood means an artery — treat it as an immediate emergency.
2. **Remember the ABCs of bleeding: Alert, Bleeding, Compress.**
   - *Why:* Alert emergency help, find the Bleeding source under clothing, and Compress it hard and without stopping. Pressure directly on the wound is the simplest and most effective way to stop it — it lets the blood clot and physically blocks the escape.
   - *Tip:* Any clean material works as a pad — a folded shirt, a towel, a scarf. If you have none, press with a bare gloved hand.
3. **Don't peek, and don't chase 'pressure points'.**
   - *Why:* Lifting the pad to check restarts the bleeding by disturbing the forming clot. Pressing on distant 'pressure points' instead of the wound is unreliable — there is usually more than one artery feeding a limb, so it doesn't stop the flow.
   - *Tip:* If blood soaks through, add more material on top and keep pressing — never remove the soaked layer.
4. **Leave embedded objects in place.**
   - *Why:* A stuck object — glass, metal, a blade — may be plugging the wound and pressing against the cut vessel. Pulling it out can release much worse bleeding you then can't control.
   - *Tip:* Pad around the object to hold it steady, and press on either side of it, not on it.
5. **Use a tourniquet only as a last resort, on a limb.**
   - *Why:* When direct pressure just won't control severe bleeding from an arm or leg, a tourniquet placed above the wound can save a life. But it cuts off all blood to the limb, so it can cause serious damage if left on too long — which is why the time must be written down and only medical staff should loosen it.
   - *Tip:* A proper manufactured tourniquet works best; improvised ones (belts, scarves) often fail to generate enough pressure.
6. **Watch for shock while you wait.**
   - *Why:* Heavy blood loss can drop the blood supply to vital organs — this is shock. Signs are pale, cold, clammy skin, fast breathing, weakness and confusion. It is a life-threatening emergency on its own.
   - *Tip:* Keep the person lying down, warm and still, and keep pressure on the wound until help arrives.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** Blood soaks through the cloth you're pressing on the wound. What do you do?
  - [✓] Add another cloth on top and keep pressing firmly
  - [ ] Remove the soaked cloth to check the wound
  - [ ] Stop pressing so a clot can form
  - *Feedback:* Never lift off to check — it disrupts clotting. Layer more material and maintain firm, steady pressure.

## 3. Burns and scalds   (`id: burns`)

*Illustration idea:* forearm held under a running tap

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Burns'; WHO burns guidance; Global First Aid Centre cooling recommendation (cool running water, at least 20 minutes).


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Stop the burning. Move the person from the heat source, put out flames, or move them away from the hot liquid or chemical.  
   *(picture: person moved away from fire)*
2. Cool the burn under cool running water for at least 20 minutes. This is the single most helpful thing you can do, and it still helps up to a few hours after the burn.  
   *(picture: burn held under running water)*
3. While cooling, gently remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells — unless they are stuck to the skin.  
   *(picture: removing a ring near a burn)*
4. Keep the person warm overall while cooling the burn, especially children — long cooling can chill the body.  
   *(picture: child wrapped in a blanket, arm under tap)*
5. After cooling, cover the burn loosely with a clean, non-fluffy cloth or clean plastic like cling film.  
   *(picture: burn covered with clean plastic)*
6. Get medical help for any burn that is large, deep, blistered, or on the face, hands, feet, joints or genitals — or any burn to a baby or elderly person.  
   *(picture: person heading to a health post)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT apply toothpaste, butter, oil, honey, herbs or ash to a burn.
- Do NOT use ice or ice-cold water — it damages the skin further.
- Do NOT burst blisters.
- Do NOT peel off clothing that is stuck to the burn.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Stop the burning before anything else.**
   - *Why:* A burn keeps damaging tissue as long as heat, hot liquid or chemical stays in contact with the skin. Removing the source — smothering flames, moving away from the heat, brushing off dry chemical — stops the injury getting deeper.
   - *Tip:* 'Stop, drop and roll' smothers clothing flames; don't run, which fans them.
2. **Cool the burn with running water for at least 20 minutes.**
   - *Why:* Cooling draws heat out of the skin, which limits how deep the burn goes, eases pain, reduces swelling and shortens healing. It still helps even if you start up to a few hours later. It is proven and costs nothing but water and time.
   - *Tip:* If clean running water is scarce, pour any clean cool water steadily over the burn — don't waste time hunting for the 'perfect' water.
3. **Cool the burn, but keep the person warm.**
   - *Why:* Twenty minutes of water on a large burn — especially on a child or a baby — can chill the whole body dangerously. You want the burn cool but the person warm.
   - *Tip:* Cover the rest of the body with a blanket while the burn is under the water.
4. **Cover loosely after cooling.**
   - *Why:* A clean, smooth covering keeps dirt and germs out and reduces pain by keeping air off the raw skin. Fluffy cotton wool sticks to the wound and must be avoided.
   - *Tip:* Clean plastic cling film, laid on (not wrapped tightly), makes an excellent burn cover if you have it.
5. **Understand why traditional remedies harm.**
   - *Why:* Toothpaste, butter, oil, honey and ash trap heat against the skin and feed bacteria, raising infection risk and making it harder for a doctor to see and treat the burn. Ice can freeze the already-damaged skin and kill more of it.
   - *Tip:* Water is the only thing a burn needs in the first 20 minutes — nothing else.
6. **Know which burns must go to hospital.**
   - *Why:* Deep burns, burns bigger than the person's palm, burns that go all the way around a limb, and burns to the face, hands, feet, joints or genitals can cause lasting harm and need medical care. So does any burn to a baby or an elderly person, and any electrical or chemical burn.
   - *Tip:* For a chemical burn, rinse with lots of running water for even longer — 20 minutes or more.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** A child spills boiling water on their arm. What is the most important first step?
  - [✓] Cool it under running water for at least 20 minutes
  - [ ] Rub toothpaste on it
  - [ ] Pack it in ice
  - *Feedback:* Prolonged cool (not ice-cold) running water is the proven treatment. Toothpaste traps heat and bacteria; ice damages the skin.

## 4. Choking   (`id: choking`)

*Illustration idea:* person bent forward receiving back blows

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Choking'; American Red Cross and British Red Cross choking guidance; Resuscitation Council UK 2025 (escalating cough → back blows → abdominal thrusts).


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Ask 'Are you choking?' If they can cough, speak or breathe, encourage them to keep coughing — a strong cough is the best way to clear a blockage.  
   *(picture: person coughing, rescuer watching)*
2. If they cannot cough, speak or breathe, or are making high-pitched sounds, act now — the airway is blocked. Bend them forward.  
   *(picture: person bent forward at the waist)*
3. Give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, one at a time.  
   *(picture: back blows between shoulder blades)*
4. If that fails, give up to 5 abdominal thrusts: stand behind, make a fist just above the navel, and pull sharply inward and upward.  
   *(picture: abdominal thrusts from behind)*
5. Keep alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object comes out or the person becomes unresponsive.  
   *(picture: alternating back blows and thrusts)*
6. If they become unresponsive, lower them gently to the ground, get emergency help, and start CPR (chest compressions) if you know how.  
   *(picture: person lowered to ground, CPR begun)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT slap the back of someone who can still cough or speak — let them cough.
- Do NOT do a blind finger-sweep in the mouth — it can push the object deeper. Only remove an object you can clearly see.
- Do NOT give abdominal thrusts to a baby under one year — use back blows and chest thrusts instead.
- Do NOT skip seeing a doctor afterward — thrusts can cause internal injury, and the airway may still be irritated.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Tell the difference between a mild and a severe block.**
   - *Why:* If they can cough, speak or breathe, air is still moving — a strong cough is the best tool and you should let them use it. If they are silent, can't breathe, or make high-pitched squeaky noises, the airway is fully blocked and they will lose consciousness within minutes unless you act.
   - *Tip:* Clutching the throat with both hands is the universal choking sign — learn to spot it across a room.
2. **Do back blows the right way.**
   - *Why:* Bending the person well forward means a dislodged object falls out of the mouth instead of back down the airway. A firm strike with the heel of your hand between the shoulder blades sends a shock wave that can free the blockage.
   - *Tip:* Give up to five, checking after each one to see if the blockage has cleared — you don't always need all five.
3. **Do abdominal thrusts the right way.**
   - *Why:* Standing behind, a sharp inward-and-upward pull just above the navel squeezes the lungs and forces trapped air upward — like a cough you create for them — which can push the object out.
   - *Tip:* For a pregnant person or someone too large to reach around, give chest thrusts instead, against the centre of the breastbone.
4. **A choking baby is handled differently.**
   - *Why:* A baby under one year is too fragile for abdominal thrusts. Instead, support them face-down along your forearm for back blows, then turn them face-up for chest thrusts using two fingers on the breastbone.
   - *Tip:* Keep the baby's head lower than the chest so gravity helps the object come out.
5. **Know what to do if they go unresponsive.**
   - *Why:* If the person collapses, the muscles relax and it may now be possible to clear the airway with CPR. Lower them gently, get emergency help, and start chest compressions — these also build pressure that can shift the object.
   - *Tip:* Only remove an object you can clearly see in the mouth. Never sweep a finger around blindly — you can push it deeper.
6. **See a doctor afterwards.**
   - *Why:* Abdominal and chest thrusts can bruise or injure internal organs, and a partly-cleared airway can still be irritated or hold fragments. A check-up is wise even when the person seems fine.
   - *Tip:* This is especially important if thrusts were needed to clear the blockage.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** A man at dinner suddenly can't speak or cough and clutches his throat. What do you do?
  - [✓] Give up to 5 back blows, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts, and repeat
  - [ ] Give him water to wash it down
  - [ ] Wait to see if it clears on its own
  - *Feedback:* A silent, non-coughing person has a fully blocked airway. Act immediately with back blows and abdominal thrusts.

## 5. Drowning   (`id: drowning`)

*Illustration idea:* person reaching a pole to someone in water

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Drowning'; WHO drowning guidance; Resuscitation Council UK 2025. DECISION: taught as trained breaths-first 30:2 with an explicit compressions-only fallback for untrained rescuers, since drowning arrest is hypoxic.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Get them out of the water without becoming a victim yourself. Reach with a pole or branch, or throw something that floats — don't jump in unless you are trained.  
   *(picture: reaching a pole from the bank)*
2. Once out, check if they respond and are breathing normally. Shout for help.  
   *(picture: checking response and breathing)*
3. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR now. If you are trained: give 2 rescue breaths first, then continue cycles of 30 chest compressions and 2 breaths. Getting air in matters most in drowning.  
   *(picture: rescue breaths being given)*
4. If you are not trained or cannot give breaths: push hard and fast in the centre of the chest without stopping. Compressions alone are far better than doing nothing.  
   *(picture: chest compressions in the centre of the chest)*
5. Send someone to call emergency services while you continue care.  
   *(picture: someone running to call for help)*
6. If they are breathing, lay them on their side (recovery position) and keep them warm — wet skin loses heat fast.  
   *(picture: person on their side, covered)*
7. Everyone rescued from drowning must see a doctor, even if they seem fine — lung problems can appear hours later.  
   *(picture: person taken to clinic)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT enter dangerous or fast-moving water to rescue someone unless trained and safe to do so.
- Do NOT try to 'push water out' of the lungs or stomach — it wastes time and can cause vomiting.
- Do NOT assume someone is fine just because they are breathing — get them checked.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Know what real drowning looks like.**
   - *Why:* Drowning is usually silent, not the splashing and shouting people expect. A drowning person often can't call out or wave — they bob at the surface, head tilted back, arms pressing down, unable to reach for help. It can happen in seconds and in shallow water.
   - *Tip:* A child who has gone quiet in or near water needs checking immediately.
2. **Rescue without becoming a second victim.**
   - *Why:* Many drowning deaths include would-be rescuers who jumped in and were pulled under by the panicking person or the current. Reaching or throwing a float is far safer and often just as effective.
   - *Tip:* Remember 'reach or throw, don't go' — a rope, pole, branch, or anything that floats can pull a person to safety.
3. **Get air in first — but never skip CPR if you can't.**
   - *Why:* Drowning kills mainly through lack of oxygen, so if you are trained, starting with 2 rescue breaths then cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths gives the best chance. This is different from a sudden adult heart attack, where compressions come first.
   - *Tip:* If you're untrained or unwilling to give breaths, don't freeze — chest compressions alone still save lives.
4. **Don't waste time trying to empty the lungs.**
   - *Why:* Pressing on the stomach or holding the person upside down to 'get the water out' does not work and delays the breaths and compressions that actually help. It also often causes vomiting, which can block the airway.
   - *Tip:* If they vomit, turn them on their side, clear the mouth, and continue.
5. **Keep a rescued person warm.**
   - *Why:* Wet skin, cold water and wind strip body heat fast, and a cold body is harder to resuscitate. Hypothermia is a real risk even in warm weather.
   - *Tip:* Remove wet clothes if you can and wrap them in something dry.
6. **Everyone rescued must see a doctor.**
   - *Why:* Even someone who seems fine after a near-drowning can develop breathing problems hours later as water and irritation affect the lungs. This delayed effect can be dangerous if missed.
   - *Tip:* Don't let a rescued person simply go home 'because they feel okay'.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** You see someone struggling in a fast river. What is the safest first action?
  - [✓] Reach or throw something that floats from the bank
  - [ ] Jump straight in to pull them out
  - *Feedback:* Reach or throw, don't go. Entering fast water risks two drownings instead of one.

## 6. Fractures and falls   (`id: fractures`)

*Illustration idea:* forearm supported in a padded splint

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Fractures, sprains and strains'; ILCOR/AHA splinting guidance (splint in position found, do not straighten).


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Keep the injured part still. Tell the person not to move it and support it in the position you found it.  
   *(picture: hand supporting an injured limb still)*
2. Steady and support the injury. If you must move them for help, splint it with padded soft cloth, tying above and below the break, not over it.  
   *(picture: padded splint being tied on)*
3. Put something cold, wrapped in cloth, on a closed injury to reduce swelling and pain. Never put ice directly on skin.  
   *(picture: wrapped cold pack on injury)*
4. If bone has broken the skin, cover the wound with a clean dressing and press gently around it — not on the bone — to control bleeding.  
   *(picture: clean dressing over an open fracture)*
5. Get to medical care. Don't let them eat or drink in case they need surgery.  
   *(picture: person transported to hospital)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT try to straighten or push a broken bone back into place.
- Do NOT move the person unnecessarily, especially if the neck or back might be hurt.
- Do NOT tie a splint so tightly it cuts off blood flow — check that fingers or toes stay warm and pink.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Recognise a likely broken bone.**
   - *Why:* You can't always be sure without an X-ray, so treat it as broken if there is severe pain, swelling, bruising, an odd shape or angle, a grating feeling, or the person can't use or bear weight on the part. A snap or crack at the time of injury is another clue.
   - *Tip:* When in doubt, treat it as a fracture — it does no harm and prevents worse injury.
2. **Immobilise the injury in the position found.**
   - *Why:* A broken bone has sharp ends near nerves and blood vessels. Straightening or pushing it can tear them and cause lasting damage. Supporting it exactly as you found it reduces pain and prevents the break getting worse on the way to help.
   - *Tip:* Pad the splint well and tie it above and below the break, never directly over it.
3. **Watch the circulation beyond the injury.**
   - *Why:* A tight splint or heavy swelling can cut off blood flow past the injury. A hand or foot that turns pale, blue, cold or numb has lost its blood supply — an emergency that can cost the limb.
   - *Tip:* Check that fingers or toes stay warm and pink; loosen the splint at once if they don't.
4. **Handle an open fracture carefully.**
   - *Why:* When bone breaks the skin, there is both bleeding and a high infection risk straight to the bone. Cover the wound with a clean dressing and press gently around it — never directly on the exposed bone.
   - *Tip:* Don't try to push the bone back under the skin.
5. **Use cold and rest to ease swelling.**
   - *Why:* For a closed injury, something cold wrapped in cloth reduces swelling and pain in the first hours. Rest and support do the same. This helps sprains and strains as well as fractures.
   - *Tip:* Never put ice directly on skin — always wrap it first.
6. **Don't move the person unnecessarily.**
   - *Why:* Especially after a fall, moving someone can worsen a hidden neck or back injury. If they may need surgery, food or drink can also delay it — so hold off on both.
   - *Tip:* Bring help and transport to the person where possible, rather than moving them to it.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** Someone falls and their forearm is bent at a wrong angle. What should you do?
  - [✓] Support and splint it in the position it's in, then get help
  - [ ] Gently straighten it back to normal
  - *Feedback:* Never straighten a fracture. Immobilise it as found to avoid tearing nerves or vessels.

## 7. Head injury   (`id: head-injury`)

*Illustration idea:* checking a dazed person after a head knock

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Head injury and concussion'; WHO / Red Cross concussion guidance. NOTE: emergency steps, 'never' items and quiz here are derived from the danger-sign guidance in the combined source topic — medical reviewer to confirm.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. After a knock to the head, sit or lie the person down, keep them still, and watch them closely.  
   *(picture: person sat down after a head knock)*
2. Watch for danger signs in the hours after: confusion, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils, clear fluid from the nose or ears, a fit, or increasing drowsiness.  
   *(picture: checking pupils and alertness)*
3. Anyone knocked unconscious even briefly, or showing any danger sign, must see a doctor urgently.  
   *(picture: person taken to a doctor)*
4. If they become unresponsive but are breathing, lay them on their side (recovery position) and watch their breathing.  
   *(picture: person on their side, recovery position)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT let someone who was knocked out 'sleep it off' without being checked by a doctor.
- Do NOT give alcohol after a head injury — it can hide worsening symptoms.
- Do NOT assume a person is fine just because they got up and walked — bleeding in the brain can appear hours later.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Watch for signs of serious head injury.**
   - *Why:* A knock to the head can cause bleeding or swelling inside the skull that builds over minutes to hours. Confusion, repeated vomiting, unequal pupils, clear fluid from the nose or ears, a bad headache or increasing drowsiness are red flags.
   - *Tip:* Anyone knocked unconscious, even briefly, must be seen by a doctor.
2. **Recognise concussion, even without a blackout.**
   - *Why:* A concussion can happen without losing consciousness. Look for confusion, memory gaps, dizziness, nausea, blurred vision or acting 'not quite right' after a head knock.
   - *Tip:* Someone with concussion should stop any activity, rest, and be watched — and see a doctor if symptoms worsen.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** After a fall, a person was knocked out for a moment but now seems fine. What should you do?
  - [✓] Have them checked by a doctor and watch for danger signs
  - [ ] Let them rest and assume it's fine
  - *Feedback:* Even a brief loss of consciousness needs medical review — dangerous bleeding in the brain can develop hours later.

## 8. Neck and back injury   (`id: spinal-injury`)

*Illustration idea:* rescuer steadying a lying person's head with both hands

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Spinal injury'; ILCOR/ANZCOR spinal guidance (manual head support, avoid rigid collars by lay providers).


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Suspect a spine injury after any fall from height, road crash, or blow to the head. Tell the person to stay still and not move.  
   *(picture: person told to stay still)*
2. Kneel behind their head and gently hold it still with both hands, keeping head, neck and body in one straight line.  
   *(picture: hands steadying the head)*
3. Keep them warm, keep talking to them, and watch their breathing while someone calls for emergency help.  
   *(picture: rescuer reassuring the person)*
4. Only move them if they are in danger (fire, water) or if they stop breathing and need CPR.  
   *(picture: person moved only for danger)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT move the head, neck or back unless life is in immediate danger.
- Do NOT bend, twist or lift the neck.
- Do NOT remove a motorcycle helmet unless you must open the airway and know how.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Know when to suspect a spine injury.**
   - *Why:* You can't see a spinal fracture, so suspect one from how the injury happened: a fall from height, a road crash, a diving accident, or any hard blow to the head or back. Pain in the neck or back, tingling, numbness or weakness in the limbs are warning signs — but a spine can be injured even without them.
   - *Tip:* When the mechanism is serious, assume a spine injury and keep the person still, even if they feel okay.
2. **Keep the head, neck and spine still and in line.**
   - *Why:* If the spine is fractured, the loose bone can press on or cut the spinal cord — the bundle of nerves controlling the body — causing permanent paralysis. Holding everything in one straight line protects the cord until trained help with proper equipment arrives.
   - *Tip:* Kneel behind the head and cradle it gently with both hands — the single most useful thing a bystander can safely do.
3. **Understand why you don't move them.**
   - *Why:* Bending, twisting or lifting the neck or back can turn a survivable injury into lifelong paralysis. This is why the rule is to steady and wait, not to sit them up, carry them, or 'walk it off'.
   - *Tip:* Leave a motorbike helmet on unless you must reach the airway and know how to remove it safely.
4. **Know the rare times you must move them.**
   - *Why:* The only reasons to move someone with a suspected spine injury are immediate danger — fire, water, traffic — or if they stop breathing and need CPR. Then, saving the life comes first.
   - *Tip:* If you must move them, try to keep the head, neck and body moving together as one unit.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** A rider is thrown from a motorbike, awake but with neck pain. What do you do?
  - [✓] Keep them still, steady their head, and wait for help
  - [ ] Help them sit up and walk it off
  - *Feedback:* Neck pain after a crash means possible spinal injury. Keep them still to avoid making it worse.

## 9. Road traffic injury   (`id: road-traffic-injury`)

*Illustration idea:* warning triangle on a road near a stopped vehicle

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 (General approach; Severe bleeding; Spinal injury) applied to road crashes; WHO road-safety and post-crash first-response guidance.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Make the scene safe first. Watch for traffic and, if you safely can, warn other drivers before you approach.  
   *(picture: warning triangle on the road)*
2. Call for emergency help and give a clear location. Ask for an ambulance.  
   *(picture: person calling for help)*
3. Do not move injured people unless there is fire or another immediate danger. Assume neck and back injuries.  
   *(picture: injured person left in place, steadied)*
4. Control any heavy bleeding with firm, direct pressure using a clean cloth.  
   *(picture: pressure applied to a wound)*
5. If someone is unresponsive but breathing, and there's no sign of spine injury, place them on their side to protect the airway.  
   *(picture: person placed on their side)*
6. Keep the injured warm, still and reassured until help arrives.  
   *(picture: person covered and reassured)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT rush into traffic — a second casualty helps no one.
- Do NOT drag injured people out of vehicles unless there is fire or immediate danger.
- Do NOT remove helmets unnecessarily.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Secure the scene before helping.**
   - *Why:* Rescuers struck by passing traffic are a real danger at crashes, and Nepal's roads add sharp bends, poor light and fast bikes. If you are hurt too, there are now more casualties and fewer helpers. Your safety genuinely comes first.
   - *Tip:* Turn on hazard lights, set out a warning triangle or object well back up the road, and have someone slow oncoming traffic.
2. **Call for help and give a clear location.**
   - *Why:* Ambulances can't come if they can't find you. A clear landmark and road name, plus how many people are hurt, gets the right help moving while you give first aid.
   - *Tip:* Send a specific person to make the call so it doesn't get lost in a crowd of bystanders.
3. **Leave people where they are unless there's danger.**
   - *Why:* Crash victims often have neck and back injuries. Dragging or sitting them up can turn a recoverable injury into permanent paralysis. Steady them in place instead.
   - *Tip:* The only exceptions are fire, sinking, or needing to reach the airway to save a life.
4. **Know your priorities: breathing, then bleeding.**
   - *Why:* After scene safety, the biggest killers are a blocked airway and heavy bleeding. An unresponsive person who isn't breathing needs CPR; heavy bleeding needs firm direct pressure. These come before minor injuries.
   - *Tip:* A quick check — are they awake, breathing, bleeding badly? — tells you what to do first.
5. **Handle helmets and riders with care.**
   - *Why:* Removing a motorbike helmet can twist an injured neck. Leave it on unless it blocks the airway of someone not breathing, and even then remove it as gently as possible, ideally with a second person steadying the neck.
   - *Tip:* An open-face helmet rarely needs removing at all to check breathing.
6. **With several casualties, help the quiet ones first.**
   - *Why:* The person screaming can breathe and has a pulse. The silent, still person may have a blocked airway or be bleeding to death. When many are hurt and help is limited, check the quiet ones first.
   - *Tip:* Enlist bystanders — one to call, others to apply pressure or steady heads under your direction.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** You arrive at a motorbike crash on a busy road. What comes first?
  - [✓] Make the scene safe and warn traffic before approaching
  - [ ] Run straight to the rider in the road
  - *Feedback:* Scene safety first. Becoming a second casualty helps no one and blocks rescuers.

## 10. Poisoning   (`id: poisoning`)

*Illustration idea:* pesticide bottle with a warning symbol

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Poisoning'; WHO guidance on poisoning and pesticide (organophosphate) exposure relevant to agricultural Nepal.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Protect yourself first. If it's fumes or gas, move the person to fresh air without breathing it in yourself.  
   *(picture: person moved to fresh air)*
2. If poison was swallowed, do not make them vomit. Find out what was taken, how much, and when.  
   *(picture: container kept for identification)*
3. Keep any container, label, plant or pill packet so medical staff can identify the poison.  
   *(picture: poison container and label saved)*
4. Call a poison centre or get to hospital immediately, and take the container with you.  
   *(picture: rushing to hospital with the container)*
5. If poison is on the skin or in the eyes, rinse with plenty of clean running water. Remove contaminated clothing.  
   *(picture: rinsing skin under running water)*
6. If they become unresponsive but are breathing, lay them on their side and be ready to start CPR.  
   *(picture: person on their side)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT make the person vomit unless a poison expert or doctor tells you to.
- Do NOT give them anything to eat or drink unless advised by a medical professional.
- Do NOT give salt water, milk, or 'home antidotes' — they can make things worse.
- Do NOT put yourself at risk from fumes or chemicals — protect yourself first.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Poison can enter the body four ways.**
   - *Why:* Poisons can be swallowed (medicines, pesticides, alcohol, plants), breathed in (smoke, fumes, gas), absorbed through the skin (chemicals, pesticides), or injected (bites, stings). Knowing the route shapes what you do — moving to fresh air for fumes, rinsing for skin contact.
   - *Tip:* Suspect poisoning when someone becomes suddenly ill near chemicals, medicines, or an unusual smell.
2. **Protect yourself first.**
   - *Why:* Fumes and chemicals that harmed the person can harm you too. If you collapse beside them, no one is left to help. Ventilate the area or move the person to fresh air only if you can do it safely.
   - *Tip:* Don't enter an enclosed space full of fumes to pull someone out — call for trained help.
3. **Never force vomiting.**
   - *Why:* This is the biggest myth to unlearn. Corrosive chemicals and petroleum products burn just as badly coming back up, and vomit can be breathed into the lungs. Making someone vomit almost always causes more harm than good.
   - *Tip:* Also skip salt water, milk and 'home antidotes' — they don't work and waste time.
4. **Take pesticide poisoning very seriously.**
   - *Why:* In farming areas, swallowed pesticide — sometimes accidental, sometimes self-harm — is a leading, fast-acting poisoning. Some pesticides (organophosphates) have specific hospital antidotes, so speed to care directly saves lives.
   - *Tip:* If self-harm may be involved, stay with the person, be calm and non-judgemental, and get both medical and emotional support.
5. **Bring the poison's identity to the hospital.**
   - *Why:* The container, label, plant or pill packet tells doctors exactly what they are treating and whether an antidote exists. Guessing costs precious time.
   - *Tip:* A clear photo of the label works too if you can't safely bring the container.
6. **Rinse skin and eyes, protect the airway.**
   - *Why:* Poison on the skin or in the eyes keeps burning until it's washed off — rinse with plenty of clean running water and remove contaminated clothing. If the person becomes unresponsive but is breathing, the side position keeps vomit from blocking the airway.
   - *Tip:* Rinse eyes from the inner corner outward so you don't wash chemical into the other eye.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** Someone swallowed pesticide. What should you do?
  - [✓] Get them to hospital fast and bring the container; do not make them vomit
  - [ ] Make them vomit to remove the poison
  - *Feedback:* Forced vomiting can cause more harm. Rapid hospital care and identifying the poison matter most — some pesticides have antidotes.

## 11. Heatstroke   (`id: heatstroke`)

*Illustration idea:* person collapsing under a hot sun

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Hyperthermia'; WHO heat-health guidance; ILCOR cooling guidance (rapid cooling for heatstroke). NOTE: one 'never' (alcohol/caffeine) is derived — reviewer to confirm.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Recognise it: very hot skin, confusion, headache, dizziness or collapse in the heat. This is life-threatening.  
   *(picture: person collapsing in the sun)*
2. Move them into shade or a cool place and remove extra clothing.  
   *(picture: person moved into shade)*
3. Cool them fast: pour cool water over the body, fan them, and place cool wet cloths on the neck, armpits and groin. Rapid cooling saves lives.  
   *(picture: cool water poured over person, fanning)*
4. If fully alert, give small sips of water. Get emergency help urgently if they are confused or unconscious.  
   *(picture: small sips of water given)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT ignore confusion or collapse in the heat — heatstroke is an emergency.
- Do NOT give alcohol or caffeine — they worsen dehydration.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Tell heat exhaustion from heatstroke.**
   - *Why:* Heat exhaustion — heavy sweating, headache, dizziness, nausea, tiredness — is a warning sign, and resting in shade with sips of water usually fixes it. Heatstroke is the emergency it can become: the person stops sweating, skin is hot and dry or flushed, and they grow confused or collapse. The body has lost temperature control.
   - *Tip:* Confusion or a change in behaviour in the heat is the line between 'rest and cool' and 'this is an emergency'.
2. **Cool a heatstroke victim rapidly.**
   - *Why:* In heatstroke every minute of high temperature damages the brain and organs, so fast cooling is the single priority — more urgent than anything else you might do. Get them into shade, strip extra clothing, and cool aggressively.
   - *Tip:* Pour cool water over the body, fan them, and put cool wet cloths on the neck, armpits and groin where big blood vessels run.
3. **Give fluids only if fully alert.**
   - *Why:* Cool water in small sips helps someone who is awake and can swallow safely. But giving drink to a confused or drowsy person risks it going into the lungs.
   - *Tip:* If they can't drink safely, focus everything on cooling and getting help.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** A farmworker collapses in extreme heat, confused and very hot to the touch. What do you do?
  - [✓] Move to shade, cool them rapidly with water and fanning, and call for help
  - [ ] Wrap them warmly and give hot tea
  - *Feedback:* Confusion plus high heat is heatstroke — an emergency needing rapid cooling, not warming.

## 12. Hypothermia   (`id: hypothermia`)

*Illustration idea:* person shivering by a snowy mountain

*Source:* IFRC Guidelines 2025 'Hypothermia'; WHO cold-health guidance. NOTE: the quiz here is derived from the topic's own guidance — reviewer to confirm.


### Emergency — What to do now (the correct path)
1. Recognise it: shivering, cold pale skin, confusion or drowsiness after cold exposure. Move them somewhere warm and dry.  
   *(picture: person shivering, being moved indoors)*
2. Remove wet clothes, wrap them in dry blankets including the head, insulate them from the cold ground, and warm them gradually.  
   *(picture: person wrapped in dry blankets)*

### Never do these (→ fatal or recoverable wrong choices)
- Do NOT give alcohol to warm someone — it actually increases heat loss.
- Do NOT rub or massage frozen or very cold limbs.
- Do NOT warm a severely cold person too fast or handle them roughly — it can trigger dangerous heart rhythms.

### Learn (use the 'why' as feedback text)
1. **Recognise hypothermia early.**
   - *Why:* Hypothermia is the body getting dangerously cold — a real risk on Nepal's high trails and cold nights, and after being wet. Early signs are shivering, cold pale skin and clumsiness; as it worsens shivering may stop and the person becomes confused, drowsy and slurred, which is more dangerous, not less.
   - *Tip:* When shivering stops but the person is still cold and confused, treat it as severe.
2. **Warm a cold person gently and gradually.**
   - *Why:* A very cold body is fragile, and rough handling or sudden intense heat can trigger a dangerous heart rhythm. Steady, gentle rewarming is safest: shelter, dry clothing, blankets, and shared body warmth.
   - *Tip:* Cover the head, insulate them from the cold ground, and remove wet clothing.
3. **Know what makes both worse.**
   - *Why:* Alcohol feels warming but actually speeds heat loss, so it harms a cold person. Rubbing frozen skin or limbs damages the tissue. And ignoring early warning signs — in either direction — lets a manageable problem become life-threatening.
   - *Tip:* Prevention matters most: shade, water and rest in heat; dry layers, shelter and fuel in cold.

### Quiz (ready-made decision points)
- **Q:** Someone is pulled from cold water, shivering and confused. How should you warm them?
  - [✓] Remove wet clothes, wrap them warmly, and warm them gradually
  - [ ] Rub their limbs hard and warm them as fast as possible
  - *Feedback:* Warm gently and gradually — rough handling or fast rewarming can trigger a dangerous heart rhythm.
